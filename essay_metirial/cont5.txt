## V. Discussion

### A. Why Fusion V2 Works

Fusion V2 combines two signals with complementary failure modes. The extended head [8] learns a discriminative boundary explicitly conditioned on the support set; it is accurate when the test-time unknown distribution resembles what was seen during training, and brittle when it doesn't. The cluster head [24] scores queries by proximity to individual support points without learning a boundary, making it less sensitive to unknown distribution shift but noisier at small K. When one signal is weak, the other often compensates.

The data reflect this complementarity. On TAU19, where per-device acoustic modes create clean gaps between known clusters, the fusion margin over the next-best single head reaches +0.035–0.050 AUROC. On TAU22, where uniform recording blurs class boundaries, the gain shrinks to +0.021. The learned weight \(\alpha\) tilts toward the discriminative signal when the boundary is sharp and toward the density signal when it is not—this adaptivity explains why fusion_v2 wins every cell of the ablation matrix, both datasets, and all way-shot/openness conditions.

A metric note: across the TAU19 ablation, AUROC spread is 0.354 vs. OSR Score spread of 40.5 pp. As a threshold-free metric [14], AUROC captures the full ranking without the variance introduced by per-round calibration at fixed TNR. We return to this in Section V.D.

### B. The wo_ood_head Surprise

The global optimum across all experiments is wo_ood_head + fusion_v2 on TAU19: AUROC = 0.985. Removing the OSR binary cross-entropy loss—the term explicitly designed to teach known/unknown separation—improves open-set detection.

Our hypothesis is gradient interference. Early in training, when the feature space is disorganized, the OSR loss pushes \(s_{\text{osr}}\) in directions that conflict with the classification gradient. The reciprocal attention [9] and separation losses already supply a weaker but less noisy separation signal. With a strong pretrained backbone [11], the additional OSR supervision adds more noise than signal.

The effect is dataset-asymmetric. wo_ood_head vs. full_model gains only +0.010 AUROC on TAU22 (0.872 vs. 0.862) but +0.013 on TAU19 (0.985 vs. 0.972). The harder dataset, where per-device variation demands more from the feature representation, benefits more from removing the conflicting gradient.

The practical moral: with a strong pretrained backbone, a well-chosen head and a clean classification loss can outperform a carefully engineered multi-term objective [5], [6]. Good features beat complicated losses.

### C. Cross-Domain Asymmetry

TAU22→TAU19 OSR Score = 90.49% vs. TAU19→TAU22 = 74.31%—a 16.2 pp gap (AUROC: 0.977 vs. 0.878). The explanation lies in target-domain feature geometry. TAU19 [19] spans three recording devices with a wide quality range, producing well-separated per-device sub-clusters with gaps between them. Unknown samples land in these gaps, making detection easy regardless of the training domain. TAU22's uniform recordings produce tighter, overlapping clusters with less open space.

The implication for practitioners: if cross-domain OSR robustness matters, train on the acoustically more diverse dataset—even at the cost of within-domain classification accuracy. The wider feature distribution acts as implicit open-set regularization. A direction we haven't explored is deliberately augmenting training with multi-device recordings or background noise to artificially widen the known-class feature distribution.

### D. AUROC vs. OSR Score as Evaluation Metrics

The choice of primary metric shapes conclusions. AUROC consistently discriminates methods better than OSR Score. The three OOD head family methods differ by < 1 pp in OSR Score but show systematic gaps of 0.005–0.010 in AUROC—small but consistent differences that track the statistical ranking. OSR Score is a point estimate at TNR = 95%; its variance includes threshold calibration noise. AUROC [14], as a threshold-free integral over the full ROC, averages this out.

Under OSR Score alone, one might conclude osr23, ext_v2, and clust_v2 are interchangeable and their ordering irrelevant. Under AUROC, the ranking osr23 ≈ clust_v2 > ext_v2 is visible across conditions, and the statistical test (Section IV.C) confirms this pattern. We recommend AUROC as the primary FOAC open-set metric, with OSR Score and TPR@TNR95 as supplementary diagnostics.

### E. Limitations

Several limitations bound the generality of our conclusions.

First, we evaluate two acoustic scene datasets from one domain family (DCASE). While TAU19 and TAU22 differ in recording conditions, they share class semantics and spectrotemporal statistics. Validation on cross-domain data—speech [31], musical instruments [30], bioacoustics—is needed.

Second, the fusion weight \(\alpha\) is a single scalar per dataset. Per-episode fusion weights conditioned on the support set could adapt to episode-to-episode variation in the known/unknown boundary.

Third, YAMNet [11] was pretrained on AudioSet [10], whose 500+ sound classes may overlap with TAU19/TAU22 categories. Known-class leakage through the pretrained backbone could inflate both classification and open-set metrics. A controlled study with a backbone trained on disjoint data would quantify this.

Fourth, we have not compared against foundation models (CLAP [15], AST [16], PaSST [28]). Whether the wo_ood_head finding holds under stronger representations is unknown.

Fifth, the near-saturation of AUROC with increasing shots (Section IV.G1) lacks a theoretical account. Why does K = 1 → 10 gain 10–15 pp ACC but under 0.02 AUROC? A model relating prototype estimation error to open-set score variance would clarify whether this is fundamental to the FOAC formulation or an architectural artifact.
