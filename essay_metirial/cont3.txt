## III. Method

### A. Problem Formulation

The FOAC task is defined over three disjoint label sets. **Base classes** \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{base}}\) contain hundreds of labeled examples per class; these are used for training the feature extractor and OSR detection heads, and are never encountered at evaluation time. **Novel classes** \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{novel}}\) are the categories the model must learn to classify from sparse support. They are partitioned into episodes, each constructed by sampling N classes and drawing K support examples and Q query examples per class. **Unknown classes** form a third label pool disjoint from both \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{base}}\) and \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{novel}}\). They appear exclusively at test time as query samples that the model must reject.

A single evaluation episode is therefore defined by a support set \(\mathcal{S} = \{(\mathbf{x}_{i}, y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N \times K}\) and a query set \(\mathcal{Q} = \{(\mathbf{x}_{j}, y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N \times Q} \;\cup\; \{(\mathbf{x}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{M}\) where the first \(N \times Q\) queries carry known-class labels and the trailing M queries are unlabeled unknowns. The M unknown samples are drawn uniformly from the unknown-class pool and are not identified as such to the model; they arrive intermixed with the known queries in random order.

The model maps each query through a shared feature extractor into an embedding \(\mathbf{h}\), then routes \(\mathbf{h}\) through two parallel heads. A **classification head** produces a distribution \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} \in \Delta^{N}\) over the N known classes via a prototype-based softmax [1]. An **OSR detection head** outputs a scalar \(s_{\text{osr}} \in \mathbb{R}\), where higher values indicate stronger evidence that the query is known. The rejection rule is:

\[
\text{decision} = \begin{cases}
\text{reject (unknown)} & \text{if } s_{\text{osr}} < \tau, \\
\arg\max_{c} \hat{y}_{c} & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]

with the threshold \(\tau\) calibrated to achieve TNR = 95% on a held-out split of the support set [5], [8]. The calibration split is disjoint from both the prototypes' support samples and the evaluation queries, which avoids the self-calibration loop that makes TNR artificially constant and uninformative.

Performance is measured along two axes. Closed-set classification accuracy (ACC) and classification AUROC (one-vs-rest) are computed over the known-class queries that survive the rejection step. Open-set detection quality is measured by the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) [14], the OSR Score—defined as \(( \text{TPR} + \text{TNR} ) / 2\) at a TNR of 95%—and TPR@TNR95, the true positive rate at the operating point where TNR equals 95%.

### B. Episodic Training Framework

Training follows the episodic meta-learning protocol [1], [2]. Each training episode is constructed to match the test-time FOAC structure: N classes are drawn from \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{base}}\), K support and Q query samples are sampled per class, and M unknown-class samples—drawn from a disjoint subset of \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{base}}\) unseen by the classifier—are injected into the query batch. The model is trained exclusively on \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{base}}\); no novel-class data appears during training. This gating ensures that evaluation on \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{novel}}\) measures generalization to genuinely unseen categories, not memorization of the base-class distribution.

**Fig 1.** FOAC episodic training architecture. An input audio clip is converted to a log-mel spectrogram and encoded by a frozen YAMNet backbone [10], [11] into a 1024-dim embedding \(\mathbf{z}\). An optional Flow Transform [17], [18] (dashed box, ~25K parameters) warps \(\mathbf{z}\) to \(\mathbf{h}\) via four affine coupling layers. The embedding then feeds two parallel branches: a classification branch (top) that computes N class prototypes and scores queries via softmax over Euclidean distances [1], and an OSR branch (bottom, expanded in the dashed inset) that routes the same embedding through one of six detection heads to produce the open-set score \(s_{\text{osr}}\). The final decision applies the rejection rule of Eq. (1): reject if \(s_{\text{osr}} < \tau\), otherwise classify.

Input audio clips are first converted to log-mel spectrograms (64 mel bands, 96 frames, 0.96 s duration) and passed through a frozen YAMNet backbone [11] pretrained on AudioSet [10]; we freeze the backbone throughout. Preliminary runs with backbone fine-tuning produced no consistent gain on TAU22 and a small degradation on TAU19, corroborating the view that AudioSet-pretrained features already span the acoustic invariances relevant to these datasets.

An optional **Flow Transform** module follows the backbone. It stacks four affine coupling layers [17], [18] that map \(\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{1024}\) to a warped representation \(\mathbf{h} = f_{\phi}(\mathbf{z})\), where \(f_{\phi}\) is invertible with a tractable log-determinant Jacobian. The flow adds approximately 25K trainable parameters. The intended effect is to Gaussianize the per-class feature distributions, which sharpens the covariance estimates that distance-based detectors rely on. Whether the flow actually delivers this benefit is dataset-dependent; the ablation in Section IV.F shows it helps on TAU22 but is neutral or mildly harmful on TAU19.

The embedding \(\mathbf{h}\)—or \(\mathbf{z}\) directly when the flow is removed—feeds two parallel branches (see Fig 1). The **classification branch** computes N class prototypes via support-set averaging and produces query class scores through a softmax over Euclidean distances to those prototypes [1]. The **OSR branch** routes the same embedding through the active detection head (Section III.C, expanded in the Fig 1 inset) to produce \(s_{\text{osr}}\). Both branches share the same feature representation; there is no separate OSR-specific feature extractor.

### C. Six OSR Detection Heads

The six heads span distinct operating principles and are shown together in the OSR branch inset of Fig 1. Each head receives the feature representation \(\mathbf{h}\) (post-flow, if the flow is active) and outputs a scalar \(s_{\text{osr}}\); heads that require per-class modeling obtain the support set's class assignments to construct their internal representations.

**1. Anti-Prototype Head (anti_proto).** Following [9], the head learns a single anti-prototype vector \(\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) that serves as a negative center for all known classes. During training, a reciprocal loss pushes \(\mathbf{a}\) away from the known-class support embeddings while the classification loss pulls class prototypes toward their respective support samples. Given N class prototypes \(\{\mathbf{p}_{c}\}_{c=1}^{N}\) computed as the mean of the K support embeddings for class c, the open-set score for a query \(\mathbf{h}\) is:

\[
s_{\text{anti}} = d(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{a}) - \min_{c \in \{1,\ldots,N\}} d(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{p}_{c})
\]

where \(d(\cdot, \cdot)\) denotes Euclidean distance. A positive score indicates the query is closer to a known prototype than to the anti-prototype and is therefore classified as known; a negative score triggers rejection. The anti-prototype is trained jointly with the backbone via the reciprocal loss described in Section III.D.

**2. Mahalanobis Distance Head (mahal).** This head fits a multivariate Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}, \mathbf{\Sigma}_{c})\) to the support embeddings of each class c [8]. The mean is the sample average of the K support vectors; the covariance is estimated via shrinkage toward a pooled diagonal matrix to stabilize inversion at small K:

\[
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{c} = (1 - \lambda) \mathbf{S}_{c} + \lambda \mathbf{\Sigma}_{\text{pooled}}
\]

where \(\mathbf{S}_{c}\) is the empirical per-class covariance and \(\lambda = 0.5\). The open-set score for query \(\mathbf{h}\) is the negative Mahalanobis distance to the nearest class:

\[
s_{\text{mahal}} = -\min_{c} \sqrt{(\mathbf{h} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{c})^{T} \mathbf{\Sigma}_{c}^{-1} (\mathbf{h} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{c})}.
\]

This head involves no learned parameters beyond what the backbone produces; it is the simplest detector in our comparison and serves as the `only_distance_head` baseline in the ablation.

**3. OOD Head OSR23 (osr23).** A Gaussian Mixture Model with \(G = 4\) components is fit to the aggregated support features of all N known classes [24]. The GMM is trained per episode via expectation-maximization initialized from k-means. The open-set score is the log-likelihood of \(\mathbf{h}\) under the mixture:

\[
s_{\text{osr23}} = \log \sum_{g=1}^{G} \pi_{g} \, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{h}; \boldsymbol{\mu}_{g}, \mathbf{\Sigma}_{g})
\]

where \(\pi_{g}\) are the learned mixing coefficients. This head models the known-class density directly rather than carving a decision boundary; out-of-distribution queries are expected to land in low-density regions under the fitted GMM.

**4. OOD Head Extended V2 (ext_v2).** This head augments the Mahalanobis detector with a learned linear transformation \(\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d'}\) that projects features to a lower-dimensional discriminative space before computing distances [8]. The open-set score becomes:

\[
s_{\text{ext}} = -\min_{c} \sqrt{(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{h} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{c})^{T} \tilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{c}^{-1} (\mathbf{W}\mathbf{h} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{c})}
\]

where \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{c} = \mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}\) and \(\tilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{c}\) is the covariance in the projected space. The matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) is trained end-to-end with the OSR binary cross-entropy loss so that the projected space separates known from unknown queries. The transformation adds roughly 300 parameters.

**5. OOD Head Cluster V2 (clust_v2).** This head takes a density estimation approach closer in spirit to osr23 but operates per-class rather than pooling across classes [24]. For each class c, the K support embeddings are treated as cluster centers; the open-set score is the negative distance to the nearest center across all N classes:

\[
s_{\text{clust}} = -\min_{c \in \{1,\ldots,N\}} \min_{k \in \{1,\ldots,K\}} \|\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{h}_{c,k}\|_{2}.
\]

When K = 1, this reduces to nearest-neighbor distance to the single prototype. When K > 1, the head retains access to individual support samples rather than collapsing them into a mean, which can be advantageous when the support set contains outlier samples that would distort a single prototype.

**6. OOD Head Fusion V2 (fusion_v2) — Core Contribution.** The fusion head (see Fig 1, bottom right of OSR inset) combines ext_v2 and clust_v2 through a learned scalar weight \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\):

\[
s_{\text{fusion}} = \alpha \cdot s_{\text{ext}} + (1 - \alpha) \cdot s_{\text{clust}}.
\]

The motivation is that ext_v2 and clust_v2 capture complementary failure modes. ext_v2 learns a discriminative boundary in a projected space—it separates known from unknown queries explicitly, conditioned on the episode's support set. clust_v2 provides a generative density estimate: queries landing far from all support points receive low scores regardless of where a learned boundary happens to fall. Discriminative boundaries tend to be brittle when the unknown distribution shifts between training and test domains; density estimates, meanwhile, grow noisy when K is small. Blending the two lets the model lean on the signal that is more reliable for a given domain and shot count. The weight \(\alpha\) is a scalar parameter optimized jointly with the rest of the model through the multi-objective loss. At inference, \(s_{\text{fusion}}\) substitutes for \(s_{\text{osr}}\) in the rejection rule of Section III.A.

### D. Training Objectives

The total loss combines four terms:

\[
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}} + \lambda_{\text{osr}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{osr}} + \lambda_{\text{rec}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}} + \lambda_{\text{sep}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{sep}}.
\]

**Classification loss** \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}\) is standard cross-entropy over the N known classes, computed on both support and query samples. For support samples, this encourages the backbone to produce well-separated class embeddings; for query samples, it drives the prototype-based classifier toward correct predictions.

**OSR loss** \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{osr}}\) is binary cross-entropy on the open-set scores [5], [6]: known queries are assigned label 1 (accept), unknown queries label 0 (reject). The loss is applied at the output of the active OSR detection head and is the primary training signal for heads that contain learnable parameters (ext_v2, fusion_v2).

**Reciprocal loss** \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}\) implements the reciprocal point constraint from [9] with weight \(\lambda_{\text{rec}} = 0.3\). It pushes the anti-prototype \(\mathbf{a}\) away from all known-class embeddings while regularizing its norm, ensuring that \(\mathbf{a}\) remains a meaningful negative reference point throughout training.

**Separation loss** \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{sep}}\) encourages the feature representations of known and unknown samples to occupy distinct regions of the embedding space [24]. It is implemented as a contrastive term with weight \(\lambda_{\text{sep}} = 0.02\) that penalizes overlap between the two distributions. Gradients are globally clipped to stabilize training, particularly during early episodes when the anti-prototype and class prototypes are poorly initialized.

To isolate the contribution of each architectural component, we define six configurations obtained by selectively removing elements from the full model. TABLE 1 lists the variants. The `full_model` includes the Flow Transform, the active OOD head, the reciprocal loss, and per-episode threshold adaptation. Each subsequent variant strips one component: `wo_flow_transform` removes the normalizing flow [17], [18]; `wo_ood_head` disables the OSR-specific training loss, retaining only classification cross-entropy and separation loss; `wo_reciprocal` drops the reciprocal attention term [9]; `wo_threshold` replaces the calibrated per-episode threshold with a fixed percentile; and `only_distance_head` reduces the system to the bare Mahalanobis detector [8] with no flow, no OOD-specific training, and no reciprocal regularization—the simplest configuration in the ablation space.

All variants share the same YAMNet backbone [11], the same episode sampling procedure, and the same optimizer settings (AdamW, learning rate \(2 \times 10^{-4}\), 200 warmup steps, 3000 training episodes). Cross-configuration comparisons therefore reflect genuine architectural differences rather than confounding factors in the training protocol.
