## II. Related Work

### A. Few-Shot Learning in Audio

Few-shot learning aims to recognize novel classes from only a handful of labeled examples, a capability that has driven substantial progress in computer vision and, more recently, in audio processing. The dominant paradigm is metric-based meta-learning, epitomized by Prototypical Networks [1], which construct a class prototype as the mean of support embeddings and classify queries by proximity in a learned metric space. Matching Networks [2] introduced the episodic training protocol and an attention-based matching mechanism over the support set. Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) [3] takes an optimization-based approach, learning an initialization amenable to rapid adaptation via a small number of gradient steps.

Subsequent work has refined these foundations along several axes. FEAT [4] augments Prototypical Networks with a set-to-set function that adapts prototype representations by attending over the support set, improving robustness to outlier samples. L³-Net [21] employs attentive graph neural networks to model inter-sample relationships within each episode. D-ProtoNet [22] explicitly minimizes prototype discrepancy across domains, while MET [23] introduces cross-attention between support and query features for finer-grained matching. More broadly, the field has explored transductive inference, data hallucination, and task-adaptive feature modulation to mitigate the data scarcity inherent to few-shot regimes.

Despite these advances, a critical limitation persists: virtually all few-shot methods, in both vision and audio, assume a closed-set evaluation protocol where every query belongs to one of the N known classes. In real-world acoustic monitoring, however, query samples routinely originate from novel sound categories not represented in the support set. The extension of few-shot learning to open-set settings—where unknown-class rejection must be performed alongside known-class classification—remains substantially under-explored in the audio domain.

### B. Open-Set Recognition

Open-set recognition (OSR) addresses the problem of distinguishing known from unknown class samples, a requirement that conventional closed-set classifiers are structurally incapable of satisfying. Early approaches repurposed the maximum SoftMax probability as a confidence proxy, rejecting low-scoring predictions as unknown [5]. While simple, this strategy is unreliable: deep networks frequently produce high SoftMax scores for out-of-distribution inputs. OpenMax [6] improved upon this by fitting Weibull distributions to extreme values of known-class logits, recalibrating the SoftMax layer to include an explicit unknown category. G-OpenMax [7] incorporated generative models to synthesize unknown-class samples for training.

Distance- and density-based methods operate in feature space. The Mahalanobis distance detector [8] fits per-class Gaussians and flags samples exceeding a calibrated distance threshold, offering a principled probabilistic interpretation at low computational cost. Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) [24] provide a more expressive alternative, modeling the known-class distribution as a mixture and using log-likelihood as the open-set score.

Prototype-based methods establish a reciprocal relationship between class representations and open-set detection. Anti-prototype learning [9] constructs a negative prototype serving as the排斥中心 for known classes; unknown samples are expected to lie closer to it than to any positive prototype. Reciprocal point learning [9] generalizes this by learning boundary-defining reciprocal points around the known-class manifold.

A critical gap runs through the OSR literature: virtually all methods assume abundant per-class training samples—typically hundreds to thousands—for learning reliable decision boundaries. In the few-shot regime, where each known class is represented by merely one to ten examples, class prototypes, covariance matrices, and GMM components all suffer from severe estimation variance. The interplay between classification accuracy and open-set detection quality under such extreme data scarcity, and the extent to which architectural choices in the OSR head can compensate for impoverished feature representations, has not been systematically characterized.

### C. Audio-Specific Considerations

Audio classification presents challenges and opportunities distinct from visual few-shot learning. Acoustic signals are represented as time-frequency spectrograms, where both temporal dynamics and spectral patterns carry discriminative information [25], [26]. Environmental sound classification must contend with variability introduced by recording devices, background noise, and reverberation—factors far less controllable than the lighting and viewpoint variations in visual recognition [19], [20].

The choice of feature extractor is consequential. YAMNet [11], a convolutional neural network pretrained on AudioSet [10] spanning over 500 sound categories, produces embeddings that capture rich acoustic semantics and generalize across recording conditions, making it a natural backbone for few-shot audio tasks. More recent architectures include Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST) [16] and its efficient variant PaSST [28], which apply self-attention to spectrogram patches. Contrastive language-audio pretraining (CLAP) [15] further aligns audio embeddings with natural language, opening possibilities for zero-shot open-set detection.

Cross-domain generalization remains a formidable challenge. The DCASE benchmarks—TAU19 [19] and TAU22 [20]—illustrate the difficulty: TAU19 was recorded across multiple devices with varying microphone quality, yielding a more heterogeneous distribution than TAU22. A model trained on one dataset and deployed on the other faces a substantial domain gap, and the interaction between domain shift and open-set detection has not been systematically characterized. This gap motivates our cross-domain evaluation protocol under bidirectional transfer.
