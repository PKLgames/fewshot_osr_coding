## II. Related Work

### A. Few-Shot Learning in Audio

Few-shot learning aims to recognize novel classes from only a handful of labeled examples. The dominant paradigm is metric-based meta-learning, epitomized by Prototypical Networks [1], which average support embeddings into class prototypes and classify queries by proximity in a learned metric space. Matching Networks [2] introduced episodic training with attention-based matching; MAML [3] learns an initialization for rapid adaptation via few gradient steps.

Subsequent refinements include FEAT [4] (set-to-set prototype adaptation), L³-Net [21] (graph neural attention), D-ProtoNet [22] (cross-domain prototype alignment), and MET [23] (support-query cross-attention). The field has also explored transductive inference, data hallucination, and task-adaptive modulation to mitigate data scarcity.

A critical limitation persists: virtually all few-shot methods assume a closed-set protocol where every query belongs to one of the N known classes. In real-world acoustic monitoring, queries routinely originate from novel categories outside the support set. The extension to open-set settings—joint classification and unknown rejection—remains substantially under-explored in audio.

### B. Open-Set Recognition

Open-set recognition (OSR) addresses known/unknown discrimination, which closed-set classifiers are structurally incapable of. Early approaches repurposed maximum SoftMax probability as a confidence proxy [5], but deep networks frequently produce high scores for out-of-distribution inputs. OpenMax [6] improved this via Weibull fitting to extreme logit values; G-OpenMax [7] added generative synthesis of unknown-class samples.

Distance- and density-based methods operate in feature space. The Mahalanobis detector [8] fits per-class Gaussians and flags samples exceeding a calibrated threshold. Gaussian Mixture Models [24] use mixture log-likelihood as a more expressive open-set score.

Prototype-based methods leverage a reciprocal relationship between class representations and detection. Anti-prototype learning [9] constructs a negative prototype that repels known-class samples; unknowns lie closer to it than to any positive prototype. Reciprocal point learning [9] generalizes this via boundary-defining reciprocal points around the known-class manifold.

A critical gap runs through the OSR literature: virtually all methods assume abundant per-class training samples for learning reliable decision boundaries. Under few-shot conditions (1–10 examples per class), prototypes, covariance matrices, and GMM components all suffer from severe estimation variance. The interplay between classification accuracy and open-set quality under such scarcity—and whether OSR head design can compensate—remains uncharacterized.

### C. Audio-Specific Considerations

Audio classification presents challenges distinct from visual few-shot learning. Acoustic signals are time-frequency spectrograms carrying discriminative information in both temporal dynamics and spectral patterns [25], [26]. Environmental sound contends with variability from recording devices, background noise, and reverberation—far less controllable than lighting and viewpoint in vision [19], [20].

The feature extractor is consequential. YAMNet [11], a CNN pretrained on AudioSet [10] (500+ categories), produces embeddings that capture rich acoustic semantics and generalize across conditions, making it a natural few-shot audio backbone. More recent architectures include Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST) [16], its efficient variant PaSST [28], and contrastive language-audio pretraining (CLAP) [15], which aligns audio with language and enables zero-shot open-set detection.

Cross-domain generalization remains formidable. The DCASE benchmarks illustrate this: TAU19 [19] spans multiple devices with varying microphone quality, yielding a more heterogeneous distribution than TAU22 [20]. A model trained on one dataset faces a substantial domain gap when deployed on the other, and the interaction between domain shift and open-set detection remains uncharacterized. This motivates our bidirectional cross-domain evaluation.
