## I. Introduction

Few-shot open-set audio classification (FOAC) addresses a dual learning problem that is both practically important and theoretically challenging: given only a small number of labeled support examples per class—typically one to ten shots—a model must simultaneously classify queries belonging to known categories and reject queries drawn from unknown categories unseen during training. This task sits at the intersection of few-shot learning [1], [2], [3] and open-set recognition [5], [6], [9], two research frontiers that have largely been pursued in isolation. In real-world acoustic scene understanding, however, the two challenges are inseparable: an environmental sound monitoring system deployed in a new acoustic environment must recognize a handful of pre-defined sound classes from sparse annotations while reliably flagging novel acoustic events that fall outside its known taxonomy. Conventional supervised audio classification systems, trained under the closed-world assumption, produce overconfident and uncalibrated predictions when confronted with unknown sound classes [5], [6], rendering them unfit for open-world deployment.

The FOAC task is compounded by three core difficulties that distinguish it from both standard few-shot classification and conventional open-set recognition. First, class prototype estimation is inherently unreliable under few-shot conditions. Prototypical networks [1] and their variants construct class representations by averaging a small number of support embeddings; with only one to five samples per class, these prototype estimates exhibit high variance, which in turn degrades both classification accuracy and the fidelity of distance-based open-set detection [8]. Second, open-set discrimination demands an explicit mechanism for separating known from unknown samples. Closed-set classifiers, by design, map all inputs onto the simplex of known classes and are thus incapable of expressing uncertainty about unfamiliar categories [6]. Third, generalization degrades under domain shift. In audio applications, acoustic scene characteristics vary substantially across recording devices, environmental conditions, and geographic locations [19], [20]. A model trained on one acoustic scene dataset may encounter fundamentally different feature distributions when deployed in another domain—for instance, when transferring from environmental sound classification to speech [25], [31]—and this distributional mismatch amplifies the difficulty of both few-shot classification and open-set rejection.

To address these challenges, we propose a unified episodic training framework [1], [2] for FOAC that integrates six complementary open-set recognition (OSR) detection heads within a single end-to-end trainable architecture. The six heads span a spectrum of detection paradigms: anti-prototype learning [9], which constructs a negative class center to repel unknown samples; Mahalanobis distance-based detection [8], which fits per-class Gaussian distributions in the feature space; OSR23, which employs Gaussian mixture models to estimate known-class density [24]; Extended V2, which learns a discriminative boundary transformation [8]; Cluster V2, which performs cluster-based density estimation [24]; and Fusion V2—our core methodological contribution—which fuses the discriminative boundary signal of the extended head with the generative density estimate of the cluster head via a learnable weighted combination. All six heads are trained jointly with a multi-objective loss comprising classification cross-entropy, open-set binary cross-entropy [5], reciprocal attention regularization [9], and a feature separation term [24].

Our contributions are three-fold. First, we conduct the first systematic comparison of six OSR detection paradigms under a unified FOAC framework, controlling for backbone architecture, training regime, and evaluation protocol. This comparison reveals substantial performance disparities: on the TAU19 acoustic scene dataset [19] under the standard 6-way 5-shot setting, Fusion V2 achieves an area under the ROC curve (AUROC) [14] of 0.985 and an OSR Score of 93.31%, outperforming the anti-prototype baseline [9] by over 30 percentage points in OSR Score and exceeding the next-best individual OOD head by approximately 0.05 in AUROC. Second, we provide a comprehensive evidence chain spanning five experimental dimensions: way-shot scaling (2–6 ways, 1–10 shots), openness analysis (2–4 unknown classes), cross-domain generalization (bidirectional TAU22–TAU19 transfer) [19], [20], component-level ablation (6 model configurations × 6 OSR heads = 36 conditions), and statistical significance testing via the Friedman test with Nemenyi post-hoc analysis [12] (p = 1.94 × 10⁻⁹). This evidence chain establishes that the choice of OSR detection head is far more consequential than architectural details, that AUROC provides stronger discriminative resolution than the widely used OSR Score [14], and that fusion-based detection yields the greatest benefit in challenging domains where the known/unknown boundary is well-separated. Third, we report a counterintuitive finding: removing the OOD-specific training loss paradoxically yields the global AUROC optimum of 0.985 on TAU19, suggesting that under sufficiently expressive features, a simple detection head with strong feature representations can outperform complex OSR-specific training objectives [5], [6].

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work in few-shot audio classification, open-set recognition, and audio-specific considerations. Section III formalizes the FOAC problem and describes the episodic training framework, the six OSR detection heads, the multi-objective training loss, and the model configuration variants used for ablation. Section IV presents the experimental evaluation across the five dimensions outlined above, including dataset details, calibration protocols, and all quantitative results. Section V discusses the implications of our findings, including the mechanism underlying Fusion V2's superiority, the surprising wo_ood_head result, cross-domain asymmetry, and the relative merits of AUROC versus OSR Score as evaluation metrics. Section VI concludes the paper and outlines directions for future work.
